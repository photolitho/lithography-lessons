    With the best possible thickness, how much does the film reflect compared with a single surface?

    A tool you need now: adding two waves

    When two waves of the same wavelength overlap, their fields add point by point. The result depends on how the two are shifted against each other: their phase difference φ. Crest on crest, they reinforce. Crest on trough, they cancel.

    Module 1 gave the bookkeeping. Each wave is an arrow, with its amplitude as length and its phase as angle. Adding waves is adding arrows, tip to tail. The length of the resulting arrow is the amplitude A of the combined wave.

    A² = a₁² + a₂² + 2·a₁·a₂·cos φ
    combined amplitudecombined intensity
    Wave 1 has amplitude 1. Intensity is given relative to wave 1 alone.
    Make the two waves cancel: combined intensity below 1%.

    Two equal waves of amplitude 1, perfectly in step. What is the combined intensity, compared with one wave alone?

    Amplitudes add; intensities do not. Two equal waves in step give four times the intensity of one, not twice. Two equal waves half a turn apart give nothing at all. No energy is destroyed: at a mirror, the light that does not come back goes forward instead.

    This is interference, and it is the whole working principle of a multilayer mirror: arrange many weak reflections so that their arrows line up.

    A² = a₁² + a₂² + 2·a₁·a₂·cos φ
    Where it comes from
    Draw the two waves as arrows, a₁ along the horizontal axis and a₂ at angle φ. Their sum has a horizontal part a₁ + a₂·cos φ and a vertical part a₂·sin φ. Pythagoras on those two parts, together with cos² φ + sin² φ = 1, gives the formula.
    Assumptions
    Two waves of the same wavelength, travelling in the same direction, with a phase difference that stays fixed in time.
    The terms
    a₁ and a₂ are the two amplitudes. φ is the phase difference between them. A is the amplitude of the combined wave, and A² its intensity.
    Check
    φ = 0 gives (a₁ + a₂)²: the amplitudes add. φ = 180° gives (a₁ − a₂)²: they subtract. φ = 90° gives a₁² + a₂²: only in that case do the intensities simply add. Try all three in the experiment.

    The phase a layer adds

    A wave that crosses a layer comes out with its arrow turned. Module 1 gave the rule: crossing a thickness d in a material of index n turns the arrow by one full turn, 2π, for every wavelength-inside λ/n that fits in the layer. Call that angle β.

    Light reflected at the bottom of a film crosses it twice, down and back up. Compared with light reflected at the top, it comes out turned by 2β.

    β = 2π·n·dλround trip = 2β = 4π·n·dλ

    nm

    A film a quarter of a wavelength thick turns the returning wave by half a turn. The wavelength meant here is the one inside the material, λ/n = 14.6 nm for molybdenum, so the quarter-wave thickness is 3.65 nm.

    This step counts only the real part of the index. The imaginary part k does its own job on the same trip: it shortens the arrow. Over 3.65 nm down and back the amplitude drops by about 2%.

    2β = 4π·n·dλ
    Where it comes from
    One turn is 2π. The number of wavelengths that fit in one crossing is d divided by the wavelength inside the material, λ/n. Multiply the two, and double for the return trip.
    Assumptions
    A film with flat, parallel faces, and light travelling perpendicular to it. At an angle the phase is smaller: d is replaced by d·cos θ, with θ the angle of the ray inside the film.
    The terms
    n is the real part of the film's index. d is its thickness. λ is the wavelength in vacuum. β is the phase of one crossing.
    Check
    d = 0 gives no turn. A film one wavelength-inside thick, 14.6 nm, gives two full turns on the round trip: set 14.6 nm and read about 720°.

    Two reflections from one film

    Now the two reflections of a molybdenum film on silicon. From module 1: the top surface, vacuum to molybdenum, reflects with r₁ = +0.040. The bottom surface, molybdenum to silicon, reflects with r₂ = −0.039. Almost equal in size, opposite in sign.

    The second wave also makes the round trip through the film, so its arrow arrives turned by 2β and slightly shortened by absorption. The reflection of the film is the sum of the two arrows.

    r ≈ r₁ + r₂ · ei·2β

    At the best thickness the round trip is about half a turn. Why does that make the two reflections add?

    Two flips make the arrows line up. You predicted: —. The minus sign of r₂ is one half turn, the round trip is the other. Together they bring the second arrow parallel to the first, and the amplitudes add: 0.040 + 0.038 = 0.078. Squared, that is 0.61%, almost four times a single surface.

    It is 3.85 times and not 4 for three small reasons you can see in the arrows. The bottom reflection is a little weaker than the top one. Absorption shortens it by another 2%. And because the indices are complex, the two arrows are never perfectly parallel.

    The peak sits at 3.61 nm, a little below the quarter-wave value of 3.65 nm. The quarter-wave rule uses only the real part of the index, so for an absorbing film it is a first estimate: the small imaginary parts tilt the arrows by a few degrees, and a thinner film absorbs less. With all bounces included the peak is 0.608%. At the 6° used in scanners, for s polarization, it moves to 3.63 nm and 0.623%.

    At d = 0 the two arrows nearly cancel, as they should. With no film the light goes from vacuum straight to silicon, which reflects almost nothing.

    r ≈ r₁ + r₂ · ei·2β
    Where it comes from
    Add the arrows of the two reflected waves. The first is r₁. The second has travelled down and back, so it carries the factor ei·2β, with the complex index inside β: the real part turns the arrow, the imaginary part shortens it.
    Assumptions
    Both surfaces are weak. Almost all the light reaches the bottom surface and comes back out, so the transmission through the top surface is taken as 1, and light that bounces more than once inside the film is ignored. Perpendicular incidence, flat and sharp surfaces.
    The terms
    r₁ is the reflection of the top surface and r₂ that of the bottom surface, each from the Fresnel formula. β = 2π·ñ·d/λ is the complex phase of one crossing.
    Check
    At d = 0 the formula gives r₁ + r₂ ≈ 0.0005 − 0.0009i, so R = 0.0001%: the tiny reflectance of bare silicon. Set the thickness to 0 and read it.

    All the bounces: the Airy formula

    The two-arrow sum left something out. The wave coming back up meets the top surface from below. A small part of it is reflected down again, bounces off the bottom once more, and so on. Each extra round trip multiplies the wave by the same factor.

    Adding infinitely many terms, each a fixed factor x times the one before, is called a geometric series. When x is smaller than 1 in size, the sum is finite:

    1 + x + x² + x³ + … = 11 − x

    Summing all the bounces this way gives the exact reflection of a single film, the Airy formula:

    r = r₁ + r₂ · ei·2β1 + r₁ · r₂ · ei·2β
    Solid: all bounces. Dashed: two arrows only.
    two arrows onlyall bounces

    A geometric series: what is 1 + 0.5 + 0.25 + 0.125 + … ?

    For the molybdenum film the two curves are almost indistinguishable. Why?

    In EUV, two arrows are almost the whole story. Each surface reflects about 4% of the amplitude, so a wave that bounces twice more is weaker by a factor 0.0015. The soap film shows the other case: each surface reflects 14% of the amplitude, and the exact curve peaks at 7.7% against 8.0% for two arrows only.

    This is why EUV mirrors are simple to reason about, and also why they need so many layers: each surface does very little.

    r = r₁ + r₂ · ei·2β1 + r₁ · r₂ · ei·2β
    Where it comes from
    Follow the wave. The first reflection is r₁. The part that enters, reflects at the bottom and leaves contributes t·t′·r₂·ei·2β. Each further round trip multiplies by −r₁·r₂·ei·2β, because seen from inside the film the top surface reflects with −r₁. Sum the geometric series and use t·t′ = 1 − r₁².
    Assumptions
    One film with flat, parallel, sharp faces. Light of a single wavelength, perpendicular to the film. Nothing else: for this ideal film the formula is exact.
    The terms
    r₁ and r₂ are the Fresnel reflections of the top and bottom surfaces. t and t′ are the transmissions through the top surface, going down and coming up. β = 2π·ñ·d/λ.
    Check
    With d = 0 the formula becomes (r₁ + r₂)/(1 + r₁·r₂). Written out with the three indices, that reduces exactly to (n₁ − n₃)/(n₁ + n₃): the film has vanished and the Fresnel formula for the two outer materials is back. When r₁·r₂ is tiny, the denominator is 1 and the two-arrow sum is recovered.

    A film too thick stops working

    Keep adding molybdenum. Every extra 7.3 nm the round trip gains a full turn and the pattern repeats: peaks where the arrows line up, dips where they oppose. But the bottom reflection has to cross more and more absorbing material.

    A molybdenum film 500 nm thick on silicon reflects about…

    Past a few hundred nanometres, only the top surface is left. The returning wave fades as e−d/L, with the same L = 167 nm as in module 1.

    The lesson for a mirror: more molybdenum does not mean more reflection. To use many surfaces the light must reach them and come back, so the absorbing layers have to stay thin, separated by something nearly transparent. That something is silicon.

    |r₂ · ei·2β| = |r₂| · e−d/L
    Where it comes from
    In module 1 the intensity fell as e−z/L over a path z, so the amplitude falls as e−z/(2L). The round trip has path z = 2d. The two factors of 2 cancel and leave e−d/L.
    Assumptions
    A uniform film and perpendicular light, as for L itself.
    The terms
    d is the film thickness. L = λ/(4πk) is the attenuation length of the film material. The left-hand side is the length of the second arrow.
    Check
    At d = 3.65 nm the factor is e−3.65/167 = 0.978: the 2% loss quoted two steps ago. At d = 167 nm it is 0.37. Set 167 nm and read 37%.

    What one film cannot do, and when these formulas apply

    The best single film reflects 0.61%. That is almost four times one surface, and still a hundred times short of a useful mirror.

    The way forward is in the arrows. One film lines up two reflections. A stack of films could line up dozens.

    Why not simply make the molybdenum film thicker to collect more reflection?

    A film's thickness varies between 3 nm and 4 nm from place to place. Can the Airy formula with one value of d describe it?

    Two films stacked: molybdenum, then silicon, on a molybdenum base. Is one application of the Airy formula enough?

    One film, two surfaces, a factor of almost four. The same idea repeated is the multilayer mirror: 40 pairs give 80 surfaces whose arrows can be lined up. Module 3 extends the bookkeeping to any number of layers.

    What to carry forward

    • A² = a₁² + a₂² + 2·a₁·a₂·cos φ. Use it whenever two waves of the same wavelength overlap with a fixed phase difference. Amplitudes add, intensities do not.
    • Round trip 2β = 4π·n·d/λ. Use it for the phase gained by crossing a flat film twice. At an angle, multiply by the cosine of the angle inside the film.
    • r = (r₁ + r₂·ei·2β)/(1 + r₁·r₂·ei·2β). Exact for one ideal film. In EUV the denominator differs from 1 by about 0.2%. It is not enough for two or more films.

    The module is closed if you can rebuild the reasoning without looking

    Why can two reflections give four times the intensity of one?

    Amplitudes add before squaring: 1 + 1 = 2, and 2² = 4.

    Where does 2β = 4π·n·d/λ come from?

    One crossing turns the arrow by 2π for every wavelength-inside, λ/n, that fits in d. The reflected wave crosses twice.

    Why is the best thickness a quarter of a wavelength?

    The bottom reflection starts out flipped. A quarter wave down and a quarter back make half a turn, which flips it again and lines it up with the top reflection.

    What does the Airy formula add to the two-arrow sum?

    All the further bounces inside the film, summed as a geometric series. In EUV they change the result by about 0.3%.

    Why does a thick film reflect like a single surface?

    The wave from the bottom surface is absorbed on its way down and back, by the factor e−d/L.
