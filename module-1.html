    How much 13.5 nm light does a smooth molybdenum surface reflect?

    First, what a light wave is

    Light is a wave: an electric field that swings up and down as it travels. Two numbers describe its shape. The amplitude A is the height of the swing. The wavelength λ is the distance from one crest to the next: 13.5 nm for EUV, about 500 nm for green light.

    A detector does not measure the field. It measures the energy delivered, the intensity I, and intensity grows as the square of the amplitude.

    I ∝ A²
    intensity
    The dashed curve is the full wave, amplitude 1.
    Set the amplitude so that the intensity is one quarter of the full wave (between 24% and 26%).

    Half the amplitude, a quarter of the intensity. This square is the reason a surface that sends back 4% of the amplitude sends back only 0.16% of the intensity.

    Now press Flipped. The wave turns upside down: crests become troughs. The intensity does not change, because (−A)² = A². A flipped wave is written with a negative amplitude. You will need this when two reflections meet.

    I ∝ A²
    Where it comes from
    The energy stored in an electric field is proportional to the field squared, just as the energy of a stretched spring is proportional to the stretch squared. Averaged over one oscillation, that leaves A².
    Assumptions
    A steady beam of a single wavelength. The constant of proportionality is left out, because this module only needs ratios.
    The terms
    A is the amplitude of the electric field. I is the intensity: power per unit area, what a detector reads.
    Check
    Amplitude 0.2 should give intensity 0.04. Set the slider to 0.20 and read 4%.

    The real part: how different the material is from vacuum

    To light, every material is a number: the refractive index n. Vacuum has n = 1. Glass, in visible light, has n = 1.5.

    What the number means: inside the material the wave keeps its rhythm, but its crests travel at speed c/n instead of c, so they sit closer together or further apart. The wavelength inside is λ/n.

    Move n and watch the reflected ray and the ray that enters.

    reflected light
    The reflected ray is drawn brighter than it really is, so you can see it.
    Make the material invisible: no reflected ray.

    Light reflects only where the two indices differ. With n = 1 the light does not notice the boundary: it goes straight through and nothing comes back.

    Notice which way the ray bends too: with n above 1 it moves toward the vertical, with n below 1 it moves away. At 13.5 nm molybdenum and silicon are both below 1.

    An index below 1 means the crests move faster than c. Crests carry no signal, so no rule of physics is broken: energy still travels slower than c.

    n₁ · sin θ₁ = n₂ · sin θ₂
    Where it comes from
    Crests arriving on one side of the boundary must line up with crests leaving on the other side, all along the surface. With a different wavelength on each side, that is only possible if the direction changes. This is Snell's law.
    Assumptions
    A flat boundary, much wider than the wavelength, between two uniform materials.
    The terms
    θ₁ and θ₂ are the angles of the two rays, measured from the perpendicular to the surface. n₁ and n₂ are the indices on the two sides.
    Check
    With n₂ = n₁ the two angles are equal: the ray goes straight, as you found at n = 1. The experiment draws the incoming ray at 30°. With n = 1.5 the law gives sin θ₂ = 0.5 / 1.5, so θ₂ = 19.5°: closer to the vertical, as drawn.

    A tool you need now: complex numbers

    The next step needs one piece of mathematics. A complex number is a pair of ordinary numbers written a + i·b, where i is a symbol with a single rule: i² = −1. Picture it as a point on a plane: a steps to the right, b steps up.

    The same point can be given as an arrow from the origin: its length, called the modulus |z|, and its angle, called the phase φ. Physics uses complex numbers because one of them carries the two things a wave has: a size and a shift.

    z = a + i·b|z|² = a² + b²
    z
    Turn the arrow until the number is −1: modulus 1, phase 180°.

    What is the modulus of 3 + 4i?

    Multiplying by −1 is a half turn. A wave whose amplitude is multiplied by −1 has its phase shifted by 180°: it is the flipped wave of two steps ago. A complex amplitude generalizes this. Its modulus scales the wave, its phase shifts it by any amount.

    One piece of shorthand: eiφ stands for the arrow of length 1 at angle φ, that is cos φ + i·sin φ. Multiplying a number by it rotates the arrow by φ without changing its length. So ei·180° = −1.

    |z|² = a² + b²
    Where it comes from
    Pythagoras. The steps a and b are the two legs of a right triangle and the arrow is its hypotenuse.
    Assumptions
    None. It holds for every complex number.
    The terms
    a is the real part, b the imaginary part, |z| the modulus.
    Check
    3 + 4i has modulus 5, because 9 + 16 = 25. In the experiment, modulus 1 at phase 90° reads 0 + 1i: one step up, none to the right.

    The imaginary part: how much the material absorbs

    The full index is a complex number, ñ = n + i·k. Here is what the second piece does. A wave that travels a distance z through a material gets multiplied by ei·2π·ñ·z/λ. Split the index into its two parts:

    ei·2π·ñ·z/λ = ei·2π·n·z/λ · e−2π·k·z/λ

    The first factor is an arrow of length 1 that only rotates: the wave oscillating, with wavelength λ/n. In the second factor the i is gone, because i · i = −1. What is left is an ordinary number that shrinks as z grows. The imaginary part of the index turns oscillation into decay.

    That second factor multiplies the amplitude. Intensity is amplitude squared, so the exponent doubles. The distance after which 37% of the intensity is left is called the attenuation length L.

    I(z) = I₀ · e−z/LL = λ4π·k
    Llight left at 500 nm

    You double k. What happens to L?

    Molybdenum at 13.5 nm has k = 0.00644. Compute L in nm.

    nm

    The larger k is, the shorter the distance light travels. It is an inverse proportion: twice the k, half the L.

    What an exponential means in practice: every stretch of length L multiplies the light by the same factor, 0.37. After 2L there is 0.37 × 0.37 = 14% left, after 3L 5%. Equal steps remove equal fractions. That is the signature of absorption.

    L = λ4π·k
    Where it comes from
    The amplitude decays as e−2πkz/λ. Squaring it for the intensity gives e−4πkz/λ. Giving the distance λ/(4πk) the name L turns that into e−z/L. So the 4π is 2π from the wave's phase, times 2 from the square.
    Assumptions
    A uniform material, light of a single wavelength, and a beam weak enough that the material treats every photon the same way.
    The terms
    λ is the wavelength in vacuum. k is the imaginary part of the index. z is the depth inside the material. I₀ is the intensity just inside the surface.
    Check
    Units: λ is a length and k is a pure number, so L is a length. Limit: k = 0 gives an infinite L, no absorption, as in vacuum. Numbers: silicon has k = 0.001826, so L = 13.5 / (4π × 0.001826) = 588 nm, the value the next steps display.

    At 13.5 nm, which of the two is almost indistinguishable from vacuum?

    Each one has a job. Molybdenum differs from vacuum, so it is the layer that reflects. Silicon has n ≈ 0.999 and absorbs little: it acts as a nearly transparent spacer.

    Why both sit just below 1: EUV light oscillates faster than most electrons in the atom can follow. The material responds weakly and slightly out of step, which leaves the index a little under 1. The tables record this as two small numbers, δ and β, with n = 1 − δ and k = β.

    Drag the slider all the way left. Below 12.45 nm the k of silicon quadruples: there the photons have enough energy to pull out a deeper group of silicon electrons, and absorption jumps. EUV works at 13.5 nm to stay on the good side of that edge.

    How much material can EUV light cross?

    Now use the real k values at 13.5 nm. Pick the material, change the thickness and see how much light comes out the other side.

    Only attenuation inside the material counts here. Reflections at the two surfaces are neglected.

    light coming outL of the material
    Find the silicon thickness that lets half the light through (between 48% and 52%).

    The halving thickness is 0.693 × L. For molybdenum, L = 167 nm. What thickness halves the light, in nm?

    nm

    About 400 nm of silicon halves the light, and 116 nm of molybdenum. A lens is millimetres thick, thousands of times more: nothing would come out. That is why there are no EUV lenses and the whole beam path is in vacuum. Only mirrors are left.

    There is good news too. Bring the thickness back to 4 nm: more than 99% gets through. A thin layer costs little, so many of them can be stacked.

    T = e−d/Ld½ = L · ln 2 ≈ 0.693 · L
    Where it comes from
    Ask for half the light: e−d/L = 1/2. The natural logarithm, ln, undoes the exponential: it answers the question "e to which power gives this number?". Applying it to both sides gives d/L = ln 2 = 0.693.
    Assumptions
    The same as for L, plus one: reflections at the two faces are ignored. At 13.5 nm each face reflects 0.16% or less, so the error is small.
    The terms
    d is the thickness. T is the fraction of light that comes out. d½ is the thickness that halves the light.
    Check
    For silicon, 0.693 × 588 nm = 408 nm: the thickness at which the goal above is reached.

    Where the Fresnel formula comes from

    You have seen the fact: light reflects only where the two indices differ. Now the reason, and the amount. Send a wave of amplitude 1 perpendicular onto a boundary. A reflected wave of amplitude r goes back, a transmitted wave of amplitude t goes on. Two things must match at the boundary.

    The field itself. The electric field cannot jump at the surface. On the incoming side it is the sum of the incoming and reflected waves, on the far side it is the transmitted wave.

    1 + r = t

    The slope of the field. How steeply the field changes along the direction of travel cannot jump either. The slope of a wave is proportional to the index of the material it is in, and the reflected wave travels backwards, so it enters with a minus sign.

    n₁ · (1 − r) = n₂ · t

    Two equations, two unknowns. Put t = 1 + r from the first into the second: n₁ − n₁·r = n₂ + n₂·r. Move the terms with r to one side: n₁ − n₂ = r · (n₁ + n₂). Divide.

    r = n₁ − n₂n₁ + n₂R = |r|²

    r is the reflected fraction of the amplitude, and it can be negative. R is the reflected fraction of the intensity, the one a detector measures.

    Air onto glass: n₁ = 1, n₂ = 1.5. What is r?

    And R, as a percentage?

    %

    4%: the reflection you see on a window. The minus sign of r says the reflected wave is flipped. It disappears in the square, but two steps from now it takes the lead.

    One more check, on energy. The first equation gives t = 1 + r = 0.8. The wave inside the glass carries n₂/n₁ times its amplitude squared, so the transmitted fraction is 1.5 × 0.8² = 0.96. Reflected plus transmitted: 0.04 + 0.96 = 1. Nothing is lost at a boundary between two transparent materials.

    r = n₁ − n₂n₁ + n₂
    Where it comes from
    Two matching conditions at the boundary, one for the field and one for its slope, solved for r.
    Assumptions
    Light arriving perpendicular to the surface. A flat, perfectly sharp boundary. Two uniform, non-magnetic materials. A single boundary: nothing behind it sends light back.
    The terms
    n₁ is the index on the side the light comes from, n₂ the index on the far side. r is the reflected amplitude relative to the incoming one; its sign, or its phase when the indices are complex, says how the reflected wave is shifted. R is the reflected fraction of intensity.
    Check
    Equal indices give r = 0: nothing reflects, as in the experiment with n = 1. A very large n₂ gives r close to −1: a perfect mirror that flips the wave. Swapping the two materials changes the sign of r and leaves R the same.

    Molybdenum at 13.5 nm reflects 0.16%

    You predicted: —. With complex indices the formula stays the same, and the arithmetic is done with complex numbers. For vacuum onto molybdenum, ñ = 0.9238 + 0.0064i:

    r = 1 − ñ1 + ñ= 0.0762 − 0.0064i1.9238 + 0.0064i≈ 0.0396 − 0.0035i

    Then the modulus squared, as in the complex-number step: R = 0.0396² + 0.0035² = 0.0016, that is 0.16%. Twenty-five times less than a window.

    Why does molybdenum reflect so little?

    At 13.5 nm the index of molybdenum is close to 1, and that of silicon is closer still. The Fresnel numerator, n₁ − n₂, is small, and squaring makes it tiny.

    Your estimate without k gave 0.156% against 0.158% for the full calculation. At this wavelength the reflection of molybdenum comes from its n; absorption adds almost nothing.

    A note on the data, at 6° and s polarization. This lesson interpolates the CXRO tables at exactly 13.500 nm and gives 0.1618%. Taking the nearest tabulated point instead, at 13.4948 nm, gives 0.1615%. Both are legitimate readings of the same tables; the choice moves the result in the fourth digit.

    When you swap the two materials, what changes?

    Same intensity, opposite sign. It follows from the formula in one line: swapping the materials swaps n₁ and n₂, which reverses the numerator and leaves the denominator alone.

    An opposite sign means the reflected wave starts out flipped: a phase shift of 180°, the half turn of the complex-number step. In a layered mirror half the surfaces reflect upright and half flipped. Added as they are, they would cancel. The layer thickness is what brings them back into agreement. That is the subject of module 2.

    What if the light arrives at an angle?

    In scanners the light hits the mirrors at about 6° from perpendicular. At an angle the same derivation goes through with one change: only the motion of the wave perpendicular to the surface matters, so each index gets multiplied by the cosine of the angle in its own material.

    rs = n₁·cos θ₁ − n₂·cos θ₂n₁·cos θ₁ + n₂·cos θ₂

    Move the angle on molybdenum at 13.5 nm. The two curves are the two polarizations: s solid, p dashed.

    R, s polarizationR, p polarization
    Find an angle at which molybdenum reflects more than 90%.

    Near grazing, even EUV reflects well. This is the principle of grazing-incidence mirrors, used in collectors and X-ray telescopes.

    Near 43° the reflection of p-polarized light reaches a very small minimum; absorption prevents it from vanishing completely.

    Forming a sharp image over a wide field, however, takes mirrors hit almost head-on. There, between 0° and 6°, R stays pinned around 0.16%.

    rs = n₁·cos θ₁ − n₂·cos θ₂n₁·cos θ₁ + n₂·cos θ₂
    Where it comes from
    The same two matching conditions as before, written for a wave that crosses the boundary obliquely. Snell's law supplies the angle θ₂ inside the material.
    Assumptions
    A flat, sharp boundary and one polarization at a time. s means the electric field is parallel to the surface. p means it lies in the plane of the incoming and reflected rays; its formula has the two indices exchanged in front of the cosines.
    The terms
    θ₁ is the angle of the incoming ray from the perpendicular. θ₂ is the angle of the ray inside, which becomes a complex number when the material absorbs.
    Check
    At θ₁ = 0 both cosines are 1 and the perpendicular formula comes back. Two landmarks of the chart can be predicted by hand. The bend near 67°: because n is below 1, Snell's law has no real solution once sin θ₁ exceeds n = 0.924, that is beyond 67.5°, and almost all the light is reflected. The dip of p near 43°: without absorption it would fall where tan θ₁ = n, at 42.7°.

    The problem to solve, and when these formulas apply

    One surface reflects 0.16% in intensity, that is |r| ≈ 0.04 in amplitude. A real mirror has 40 Mo/Si pairs: 80 surfaces.

    If all 80 reflections added up in agreement, the amplitude would be 80 × 0.04 = 3.2 and the reflectance would exceed 1000%.

    That is impossible. What prevents it?

    A silicon wafer carries a molybdenum film 3 nm thick. Can the single-surface formula alone give its reflectance?

    A surface has bumps 2 nm high. For 13.5 nm light, which assumption of the Fresnel formula fails?

    The sum saturates. The nominal model, an ideal stack of 40 pairs with perfectly sharp and smooth interfaces, reaches 74.2%. That number comes from a transfer-matrix calculation, the method the next modules build up to. Real mirrors reflect somewhat less, because their interfaces are not perfect.

    The single-surface formula is the building block. What it cannot do alone is add reflections from several surfaces: for that you need the phase each wave picks up while crossing a layer.

    What to carry forward

    • I(z) = I₀ · e−z/L, with L = λ/(4πk). Use it for light crossing a uniform material. It is not enough for a layer so thin that reflections between its two faces matter.
    • r = (n₁ − n₂)/(n₁ + n₂), R = |r|². Use it for one flat, sharp boundary hit perpendicularly; at an angle, add the cosines. It is not enough for a rough surface or for several boundaries at once.
    • Swapping the materials flips the sign of r. True at every single boundary, and the key to stacking layers.

    The module is closed if you can rebuild the reasoning without looking

    What do n and k represent?

    n sets the speed of the crests in the material, and so how much the material bends and reflects light. k sets how fast the wave decays: how much the material absorbs.

    Where does the 4π in L = λ/(4πk) come from?

    2π from the phase of the wave, and a factor 2 because intensity is amplitude squared.

    Which two conditions give the Fresnel formula?

    At the boundary the field must be continuous, 1 + r = t, and so must its slope, n₁(1 − r) = n₂t. Solving the pair for r gives the formula.

    Why does EUV work at 13.5 nm and not at 12 nm?

    Below 12.4 nm silicon starts absorbing much more. At 13.5 nm it is nearly transparent and acts as a spacer.

    Why are there no EUV lenses?

    Light is halved in about 400 nm of silicon and in 116 nm of molybdenum. A lens is thousands of times thicker.

    How much does a molybdenum surface reflect, and why so little?

    0.16%. Its n is only 0.076 away from 1, and Fresnel squares that difference.

    What is the difference between Si→Mo and Mo→Si?

    Same R, but r has the opposite sign: the reflected wave starts out flipped.
