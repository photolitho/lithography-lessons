    How far can the wavelength move from the best one before the reflectance drops to half?

    A mirror only for a band of wavelengths

    Here is the reflectance of the mirror of module 3 against wavelength, computed with the matrix method. At each wavelength the indices of molybdenum and silicon are read again from the tables, because they change with wavelength too.

    The width of the peak is measured at half its height: find the highest point, take half of that value, and measure the distance between the two wavelengths, one on each side, where the curve crosses it. This is the full width at half maximum, FWHM.

    R at the cursorpeak atFWHM0.886 · λ / N
    Molybdenum 2.78 nm on silicon 4.18 nm, repeated, at 6° and s polarization.
    Set 40 pairs and put the cursor on the peak (R above 74.2%).

    For 5 pairs the simple estimate of the width is 0.886 · λ / N. With λ = 13.5 nm, what is it in nm?

    nm

    With 40 pairs the peak is 0.64 nm wide and sits at 13.53 nm. You predicted: —. The reflectance stays above half its peak value between 13.14 and 13.77 nm. The peak is not symmetric: those two points lie 0.39 nm below it and 0.25 nm above.

    The reason is in the arrows. Away from the best wavelength, each pair's arrow is turned a little against the previous one. Over a few pairs that hardly matters. Over many it adds up, the chain curls, and the sum collapses. The more pairs take part, the smaller the detuning that is tolerated.

    With 5 pairs the simple estimate and the full calculation agree: 2.39 nm. With 40 pairs the estimate says 0.30 nm and the calculation 0.64 nm. The estimate counts all 40 pairs as equal partners, and module 3 showed they are not: the deep ones receive little light. Read through this estimate alone, 0.64 nm is the width that about 19 equally weighted pairs would give. The other pairs still contribute, only less and less, which is why adding more hardly narrows the peak.

    |sum| = | sin(N·ε/2)sin(ε/2) |FWHM ≈ 0.886 · λN
    Where it comes from
    Add N arrows of length 1, each turned by the same small angle ε against the one before. This is a finite geometric sum, 1 + x + … + xN−1 = (1 − xN)/(1 − x), with x = eiε. Unlike the infinite series of module 2 it needs no condition on the size of x, only x ≠ 1. Its length is the ratio shown. Detuning the wavelength by Δλ turns each pair's arrow by ε = 2π·Δλ/λ. For small ε, sin(ε/2) ≈ ε/2, so the intensity goes as (sin u / u)² with u = N·ε/2, and falls to half at u = 1.39. That gives the half-width Δλ = 1.39·λ/(π·N). The full width is twice that: 0.886·λ/N.
    Assumptions
    N identical pairs that all contribute equally: weak reflections and no absorption. Indices that do not change over the width of the peak.
    The terms
    N is the number of pairs. ε is the extra turn from one pair to the next. λ is the wavelength of the peak. FWHM is the full width of the peak at half its height.
    Check
    With ε = 0 the ratio becomes N: all arrows in line. It first reaches zero when N·ε = 2π: the chain has closed into a full circle. For 5 pairs the estimate gives 2.39 nm, and so does the experiment. For 40 pairs it fails, as it should, because its first assumption fails.

    The period sets the wavelength

    The Bragg condition ties the best wavelength to the period: λ = 2·D·n̄·cos θ. A thicker period should move the whole peak to longer wavelengths, in proportion.

    The period grows by 1%. By the Bragg condition, how many nm does a peak at 13.5 nm move?

    nm

    A period of 6.948 nm puts the peak at 13.50 nm. That is 2.78 nm of molybdenum and 4.17 nm of silicon. This is a nominal value. How closely a real coating has to match it depends on how much loss of reflectance, and how much optical error, can be accepted.

    The proportion holds, roughly. Going from 6.96 to 7.03 nm is a 1% change, and the peak moves from 13.53 to 13.65 nm: 0.12 nm, or 0.9%, instead of the 0.135 nm of the simple rule. The difference comes from the materials. At longer wavelengths the index of molybdenum is lower, so n̄ falls as the peak moves, and the peak moves a little less.

    There is also a constant offset. For D = 6.96 nm the Bragg formula predicts 13.41 nm, and the full calculation gives 13.53 nm. The formula is a guide to how the peak moves, and a first estimate of where it is. The design value has to come from the full calculation.

    Δλλ ≈ ΔDD
    Where it comes from
    In λ = 2·D·n̄·cos θ the wavelength is proportional to D. Multiply D by 1.01 and λ is multiplied by 1.01.
    Assumptions
    The share of molybdenum and the angle stay the same. The average index n̄ does not change between the old and the new wavelength.
    The terms
    ΔD is the change of the period and Δλ the resulting change of the peak wavelength. Both are taken relative to their starting values.
    Check
    From 6.96 to 7.03 nm the rule predicts +0.135 nm. The experiment gives +0.12 nm. The second assumption is the one that is not quite met.

    The same mirror at another angle

    The Bragg condition contains the angle too. Light that crosses the stack obliquely has a shorter wave motion perpendicular to the layers, by the factor cos θ, so the same period fits a shorter wavelength.

    λpeak(θ) ≈ λpeak(0) · cos θ

    You tilt the mirror further away from perpendicular. Where does the peak go?

    A mirror is made for one wavelength at one angle. This stack reflects 13.5 nm best at about 7°. At perpendicular incidence its peak is at 13.60 nm, and 13.5 nm is reflected at 72.8%. At 15° the peak is at 13.16 nm, and 13.5 nm is almost not reflected at all: 9%.

    In a scanner different mirrors are hit at different angles, and each needs its own period. Where the angle of incidence varies across a curved mirror, the period of the coating can be graded across the surface to keep it tuned.

    λpeak(θ) ≈ λpeak(0) · cos θ
    Where it comes from
    From λ = 2·D·n̄·cos θ with D and n̄ fixed. Module 2 gave the reason for the cosine: at an angle the phase gained crossing a layer is 2π·n·d·cos θ/λ.
    Assumptions
    θ is the angle inside the stack. Because n̄ is close to 1 it is nearly the angle in vacuum, and the formula uses that one. The average index does not change over the shift.
    The terms
    λpeak(0) is the peak wavelength at perpendicular incidence, 13.60 nm for this stack. θ is the angle from the perpendicular.
    Check
    At 10° the formula gives 13.60 × cos 10° = 13.39 nm, and the experiment 13.40 nm. At 20° it gives 12.78 nm against 12.83 nm: the approximations start to show.

    How much molybdenum in each pair?

    The period fixes the wavelength. It does not say how to share the period between the two materials. Call Γ the fraction taken by molybdenum: Γ = dMo / D.

    A pair has two surfaces that reflect with opposite signs. Module 2 found when two such reflections add best: when the round trip between them is half a turn. With one full turn per pair, half a turn means the molybdenum layer takes about half of the period.

    arrow of one pair ∝ 2 · sin(π · Γ)
    Height of the reflectance peak against the share of molybdenum, for 40 pairs with a period of 6.96 nm.
    peak R2 · sin(π · Γ)
    With real materials, find the share of molybdenum that gives the highest peak (above 74.25%).

    Without absorption the best share is close to 0.5. With real materials it is 0.4. Why does it move?

    A compromise between lining up the arrows and losing light. Shares near one half make each pair's arrow as long as it can be. But molybdenum is the absorber, and every nanometre of it is crossed twice by all the light that goes deeper. In the simple model, going from 0.5 to 0.4 gives up 5% of the arrow, since sin(0.4·π) = 0.951. With real materials it gains more than it costs: the peak rises from 73.2% to 74.3%.

    The same curve shows how forgiving the choice is. Between 0.35 and 0.45 the peak stays within half a point of its best value. The share is far less critical than the period.

    arrow of one pair ∝ 2 · sin(π · Γ)
    Where it comes from
    Take the two surfaces of the molybdenum layer. Their reflections are +r and −r. The second has crossed the layer twice, a round trip of 2π·Γ when the whole pair makes one turn. The sum r·(1 − ei·2πΓ) has length 2·r·sin(π·Γ).
    Assumptions
    Weak reflections and no absorption, so that one pair can be looked at alone. The two indices are close enough that the share of thickness is also the share of phase.
    The terms
    Γ = dMo/D is the fraction of the period taken by molybdenum. r is the size of the reflection of one surface.
    Check
    In this simple model Γ = 0.5 gives 2: two quarter-wave layers, the best case of module 2. Γ = 0 or 1 gives 0: with a single material the repeated boundaries between molybdenum and silicon are gone, and with them the Bragg contribution. Only the outer boundaries of the coating remain. In the experiment without absorption the curve peaks near 0.5; not exactly, because with two different indices equal thicknesses are not quite equal phases.

    What this means for a real machine

    The mirror reflects a band 0.64 nm wide, centred where the period and the angle put it. Three consequences, each a use of what this module built.

    Consider eleven such mirrors in a row, each reflecting 74.2%. What percentage of the light is left after all eleven?

    %

    Someone proposes 200 pairs to get a very narrow band, quoting 0.886 · λ / N = 0.06 nm. Will it work?

    A mirror warms up and its period grows by 0.1%. How serious is the shift of the peak?

    Eleven mirrors pass less than 4% of the light. This is why every point of reflectance matters, and why the sources of EUV scanners have to be so powerful. It is also why the 26% that each mirror does not reflect matters: it ends up as heat in the mirrors. Module 5 follows that light.

    For this small, uniform change the reflectance hardly moves. Heating can also distort the surface of a mirror and introduce wavefront errors, which is a separate optical problem.

    What to carry forward

    • FWHM ≈ 0.886·λ/N. Use it when all N pairs contribute equally: few pairs, weak reflections. For a full EUV mirror it underestimates the width by a factor of two.
    • Δλ/λ ≈ ΔD/D and λpeak ∝ cos θ. Use them to estimate how the peak moves with period and angle. They are not accurate enough to place the peak: that takes the full calculation.
    • Arrow of one pair ∝ 2·sin(π·Γ). Use it to see why equal shares line the arrows up best. It ignores absorption, which is what moves the real optimum to 0.4.

    Why does the mirror reflect only a band of wavelengths?

    Off the best wavelength each pair's arrow is turned a little against the previous one. Over many pairs the chain curls and the sum collapses.

    How is the FWHM defined?

    Take half the height of the peak and measure the distance between the two wavelengths, one on each side, where the curve crosses that value.

    Why is the real band twice as wide as 0.886·λ/N says for 40 pairs?

    The estimate assumes 40 equal pairs. Reflection and absorption give the deep ones less light, so the peak is as wide as about 19 equal pairs would make it.

    What moves the peak, and which way?

    A thicker period moves it to longer wavelengths, in proportion. A larger angle from perpendicular moves it to shorter wavelengths, as cos θ.

    Why is the share of molybdenum 0.4 and not 0.5?

    Equal shares give the longest arrow per pair, but molybdenum absorbs three and a half times more than silicon. Slightly less of it gains more than it costs.
